How the Time Lab works, step by step

What happens between typing a position and seeing a moving dot: where the data come from, what is done to them, what already existed and what we added, and the papers that describe SPHEREx itself.

Background

What SPHEREx records

Survey

Four all-sky maps

Launched on 11 March 2025, SPHEREx began science operations on 1 May 2025. Over its 25-month prime mission it maps the whole sky four times, roughly once every six months, with much deeper coverage near the ecliptic poles.

Spectra

102 channels, 0.75–5 µm

Six H2RG detectors sit under linear variable filters, so the wavelength changes across each detector and every pixel samples one wavelength. Resolving power is about 35–130. Detector pairs 1–4, 2–5 and 3–6 see the same sky at once.

Pixels

6.15″ per pixel

Each detector covers about 3.5° × 3.5°. The point spread function is undersampled by design: two stars closer than a few arcseconds blend into one spot, so precise positions come from Gaia, while SPHEREx measures brightness.

Our steps

From a position to a measurement

  1. Choose a field

    The field centre is an ICRS position in degrees. Names are resolved with CDS Sesame; if the main CDS host does not answer, two VizieR mirrors are tried. The five challenge test targets start from catalogue positions moved with their proper motions to the middle of the search window, so that fast stars stay inside the field.

  2. Find exposures

    The lab queries the IRSA image service for SPHEREx Data Release 1 (or Quick Release 3) spectral images covering the position, for one detector and a range of UTC dates. When there are more matches than the series limit, frames are picked evenly in time and the rest are listed as excluded.

  3. Read the calibrated FITS files

    Each cutout keeps the archive’s calibrated image in MJy/sr, its per-pixel quality flags, the variance map and the spectral WCS that gives the wavelength of every pixel. Nothing is measured on display images.

  4. Put frames on one grid

    For display, all frames are resampled onto one north-up tangent-plane grid of 256 × 256 pixels and drawn with the same asinh brightness scale (limits from the 12th and 99.7th percentiles of the whole series). A shared scale means that a change in brightness on screen is not caused by the display.

  5. Blink

    Frames play in UTC order. Because of the linear variable filters, a position is usually seen at a different wavelength on each date, so the lab shows the time and wavelength difference between any two frames.

  6. Subtract proposed by us

    The difference view converts the grey levels of both frames back to MJy/sr through the shared stretch and subtracts them. Pixels flagged in either frame, missing, or saturated by the stretch are excluded and shown as a checker pattern. Fixed stars cancel; a moving object leaves a dark spot where it was and a bright spot where it is.

  7. Overlay Gaia DR3

    Gaia DR3 positions (epoch J2016.0) are moved with constant proper motion to the time of each frame. In the difference view they are drawn for both dates, so a proper-motion pair lines up with two circles. Parallax is not applied; for these targets it is below one SPHEREx pixel.

  8. Measure

    In the science lab, a pixel-convolved point spread function and a planar background are fitted to the original pixels, weighted by the variance map. Results come with flux errors, positions, the wavelength of the fit and a quality flag (reduced χ² ≤ 3 and signal-to-noise ≥ 5). The same measurements build spectra and light curves.

  9. Check candidates

    A track is associated with a Gaia DR3 star only if one star lies within the search radius at every detection epoch, with at least three independent epochs. Known asteroids and comets are checked against JPL predictions computed from the spacecraft’s own position.

  10. Keep the provenance

    Every series records archive queries, dataset DOIs, calibration and method versions, file checksums and its stated limitations, and can be downloaded as a record.

Before and proposed

What already existed, and what we added

Already available

At IRSA

  • DR1, QR2 and QR3 spectral images, the DR1 all-sky cube and its HiPS maps.
  • SPHEREx Data Explorer for searching and viewing images.
  • Spectrophotometry, Cutout, Mosaic and Source Discovery tools.
  • The Moving Object Search Tool (MOST) for known solar system objects.
  • Python notebooks and cloud access on AWS.

In the first version of the lab

  • Sky atlas on the DR1 cube, archive search and blink player.
  • Side-by-side comparison of two frames and a quality-flag overlay.
  • Gaia DR3 overlay, point-source photometry, spectra and light curves.
  • Candidate tracks, local sessions, deep links and embedding.
What we proposed

Merged into the lab

  • newDifference images in MJy/sr, with Gaia drawn at both epochs.
  • newOne-click presets for the five challenge test targets, with proper motion applied.
  • newName lookup that falls back to Sesame mirrors.

In this round

  • Montserrat and JetBrains Mono, a blue accent, and aligned icons.
  • An overview page, this method page and an About page.

Next steps

  • Stacking all frames of one visit for better signal-to-noise.
  • Adjustable brightness stretch and an AllWISE overlay.
  • Injecting fake movers to measure how faint and slow an object the lab can still recover.

IRSA’s tools serve researchers who already know what they are looking for. The Time Lab adds one fast path for everyone: enter a position, watch it change, and see whether the change is real.

Limits

What the lab does not claim

  • Display images are resampled for viewing only; numbers come from the original pixels.
  • Frames from different dates usually sample different wavelengths, so stars of different colours can leave residuals in a difference image even if nothing changed.
  • Photometric errors include pixel noise but not calibration systematics.
  • Associations with Gaia are positional, not confirmed identities. Candidates are for further checking, not discoveries.
  • Over the first year and a half, Barnard’s Star moves about 2.5 SPHEREx pixels and Epsilon Indi B about one, so motion is clearest between the first and last frames.

Canonical papers

The papers behind SPHEREx

IRSA lists four canonical papers for the mission. Summaries below are ours; follow the links for the abstracts and full texts.

Mission

The SPHEREx Satellite Mission

Bock, J. J. et al. 2026, ApJ, 999, 139 · ADS · arXiv:2511.02985 · DOI

The overview of the first all-sky near-infrared spectral survey: 102 bands from 0.75 to 5.0 µm at resolving power 35–130 in 6.2″ pixels. After four full-sky maps over two years the expected 5σ point-source depth is about 19.5–19.9 AB mag below 3.8 µm and 17.8–18.8 AB mag above it. The paper connects the three science themes (inflation, galaxy evolution through intensity mapping, interstellar ices) to the design of the instrument and spacecraft and reports first in-flight performance.

Pipeline

The SPHEREx Image and Spectrophotometry Processing Pipeline

Akeson, R. et al. 2026, ApJS, 285, 67 · ADS · arXiv:2511.15823

How the SPHEREx Science Data Center at Caltech/IPAC turns raw exposures into flux- and wavelength-calibrated spectral images and photometry, and which data products reach the archive. This is the source of the images, flags, variance maps and spectral WCS that the Time Lab reads.

Sky Simulator

The SPHEREx Sky Simulator

Crill, B. P. et al. 2025, ApJS, 281, 10 · ADS · arXiv:2505.24856

A model of what SPHEREx would see, built from astrophysical emission, instrument properties and the survey plan, including noise and systematics. It was used before launch to predict science performance and to develop the pipeline. The pre-launch 5σ sensitivity estimate was about 18.5–19 AB mag from 0.75 to 3.8 µm and 16.6–18 AB mag from 3.8 to 5 µm, limited by zodiacal light.

Instrument & calibration

The SPHEREx Instrument: calibration, testing and performance

Korngut, P. M. et al. 2026, arXiv:2603.29835 · ADS · arXiv

The full test campaign of the instrument, from individual components in the laboratory to in-orbit commissioning, traced back to the requirements of each science goal. The authors report that the instrument, now in full survey mode, meets its requirements for optical performance, point-source sensitivity, thermal stability and correlated noise.
